# DEAD WAX — Combat Feel (v1, 2026-08-08)

*The identity doc for how fighting feels. Locks the four dials Gabe chose and reconciles them into one coherent thing. Numbers are M1 units; the "→" values are the retune this identity implies (all knobs, verify in play).*

## The one-line identity

**Sekiro in a music box.** Heavy, deliberate, tight and tense — combat is percussion played on a slow, weighty beat, where a health bar and a posture-like resonance meter both matter, the beat *pressures* you without locking you, and the single best-feeling action is the parry: you catch a blow and play it back.

## The four dials (locked)

| Dial | Choice | What it means for feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Rhythm** | Groove-*pressured* | Off-beat still works but is *noticeably* weaker; the game leans on you to find the pocket. Not a rhythm game — a rhythm-*aware* one. |
| **Weight** | Deliberate & heavy | Every swing commits. Spacing and patience win; greed dies. The beat is SLOW (a doom-riff pocket, not breakcore) so weight and rhythm coexist. |
| **Difficulty** | Hard — tight & tense | Small windows, high punish. Bosses are walls you learn. Mercy is the pressure valve, not the easy button. |
| **Damage** | HP + resonance | Two bars, Sekiro-shaped: chip HP the slow way, or build resonance to **shatter** (an execute). Parries are the fast lane to shatter. |

These four aren't a grab bag — they interlock. Heavy+deliberate makes a *slow* beat, which is what lets groove-pressure coexist with weight (you're finding a heavy pocket, not tapping fast). Hard+tight makes the two-bar model shine (a pure-HP grind would be tedious at this punish level; resonance gives skilled players a fast, spectacular out). And a tight punishing game needs one release-valve action that feels *incredible* — the parry — and one thematic off-ramp — mercy. Both are load-bearing precisely *because* it's hard.

## The moment-to-moment loop

1. **Read the room by ear.** Telegraphs are heard 4-8 frames before they're seen (ENEMIES.md law 1). In a hard game this is the fairness contract: every death is a tell you missed, never a tell that didn't exist.
2. **Choose to break silence.** Striking is loud; the first blow commits you to the room (noise economy). In a deliberate game, *starting* a fight has weight — the quiet before the first hit is real tension.
3. **Work the heavy pocket.** Grounded strikes are committed and slow; landing them on-beat hits meaningfully harder (groove pressure). Off-beat panic-mashing chips a little and builds nothing — the game *sounds* worse when you play worse, and plays worse too.
4. **Ride the pendulum.** Strike-launches throw you airborne — the release after the heavy grounded commits. Aerial gives you reposition and a beat to breathe before you plant again. Heavy → heavy → *float* → heavy.
5. **Two ways to win.** Chip HP the patient way, or spike resonance with hits and especially **parries** until it caps and you **shatter** them — a burst execute, the drop, the word-splatter. The skilled, aggressive, on-beat, parry-heavy player wins *fast and beautifully*; the careful player wins *slow and safe*. Both valid; the game rewards the flex without requiring it.
6. **Or don't win at all.** SET-play the mercy exit — but see below, because in a hard game that's the hardest thing you can do.

## The parry — the heart, specified

The single most important feel in the game. Everything about it says *this is the move*:

- **Window:** 130ms (M1) → **~100ms**. Tightened for a hard game, but kept the *most generous* of the tight windows, because it's the heart and it must feel learnable, not luck.
- **Payoff:** the biggest in the game. Resonance +0.35 (M1) → **+0.40**, full enemy stagger, and it **costs the enemy nothing you have** — no health risk, pure profit for skill. A parry chain is the fastest route to a shatter.
- **Sound:** the enemy's own attack, pitched and played back as a clean note. A great parry *sounds* like catching music. This is the audio team's single most important cue.
- **Theme:** it is the one combat action that isn't destruction — it's *reflection*. You give a thing its own sound back. The game about mercy makes its best move a kind of listening. Lean into this everywhere: the parry is joy; everything else in combat carries a little weight.

## Mercy in a hard game — the reconciliation (this is the good part)

The instinct is that "hard combat" and "you can always choose mercy" fight each other. They don't — a hard game makes mercy *mean* something:

**SET-play is defenseless and slow. In a forgiving game that's trivial. In a tight, punishing game, creating enough safety to kneel and hold a bar is the hardest, highest-skill thing you can do.** You have to *earn* the opening — stagger the room, parry into space, clear the adds — and then choose to spend that hard-won safety on kindness instead of a kill. Mercy becomes a *flex*, the mark of a player good enough that they don't *need* to fight but choose peace anyway. In a cruel-feeling world, being gentle is the master move. The difficulty doesn't undercut the theme — **it proves it.** The player who mercies the whole deep on a hard run has done something genuinely hard, and the B5 audit knows.

This also means mercy scales right: trivial early (easy rooms, easy to kneel), brutal late (the Pressed, three defenseless bars in Dead Wax). The cost curve of kindness *is* the difficulty curve. They were never two systems.

## Winning feels a little sad (the emotional texture, locked)

Every shatter spills the words the thing was mid-saying (word-splatter). Even a flawless execute has a hair of sorrow under the satisfaction — you ended something that was trying to speak. The parry is the only pure-joy release, *because* it doesn't kill. Net effect: combat that feels *good to be good at* and *never triumphant*. Bosses especially: no victory fanfare, no fist-pump — a beat of quiet, the book's small reaction, and forward. Save the triumph for the ending; the fights are elegies you happen to win.

## The depth arc — combat feel changes as you descend

Depth re-tunes (ENEMIES.md law 4). Across the whole game the *feel* of fighting shifts, which keeps a hard combat system from going stale:

- **The Label / Overture (dry):** short, snappy, percussive. Dead air = strikes die close, shockwaves don't carry. Knife-fight range, fast reads, the crunchiest and most immediate combat. Where the player learns to be precise.
- **The Unplayed (thick):** strikes bloom and carry; launches go big. Combat opens up into spacing and aerials — the pendulum at its widest. More room, more air, more flow.
- **The Undersong (liquid):** slow, floating, heavy-hitting. You swim; everything has weight and delay; fights become an underwater ballet where commitment is everything and mistakes are slow to take back. The most dreamlike and the most punishing.
- **Dead Wax (pressed):** minimal, enormous, final. The Pressed barely move; hits land like tectonics. Combat as ritual, every blow a decision. The game's fighting, slowed to a heartbeat.

The game gets *dreamier and heavier* the deeper you go — the combat texture itself descends.

## Feel targets (what "good" means here)

- A parry should feel like **catching a knife and it turning into a bell-note.**
- A heavy grounded strike should feel like **it costs something to throw** — no free swings, real recovery, real commitment.
- A shatter should feel like **a drop** — build, build, release; the loud beautiful punctuation the whole fight was reaching for.
- Starting a fight should feel like **the first hit on a drum in a silent room** — a decision with weight, breaking a quiet you were keeping.
- A cleared room should feel like **the quiet coming back** — the deep calming as you master it.
- Mercy should feel like **the hardest, most graceful thing you know how to do.**

## Anti-goals (what combat must NOT feel like)

- **Not mashy.** Weight + groove-pressure + real recovery kill the mash. If you can button-spam and win, the tuning failed.
- **Not weightless.** Skip is heavy; if a swing feels free and floaty, it's wrong.
- **Not unfair.** Hard is a promise that every death was *readable* — the audio-first telegraph is the contract. Fake difficulty (unseen hits, unreadable tells) breaks the whole thing.
- **Not triumphant.** No victory fanfares on kills. The sadness under the satisfaction is the point.
- **Not divorced from sound.** If a fight could be muted and play identically, we've lost the identity. The beat, the pitched-back parry, the crackle, the shatter-words — combat *is* the music.

## M1 deltas (what this identity changes in the prototype)

M1 was built groove-*rewarded* and resonance-*only*. This identity needs:

1. **Add enemy HP** alongside resonance (test_pressing.gd is resonance-only today). Two bars: HP chips, resonance builds to a shatter-execute. Shatter should now *finish from any HP* (execute) — Sekiro deathblow, not just extra damage.
2. **Groove pressure:** off-beat strikes ~0.6x damage/resonance; on-beat unchanged. Make the difference *felt* (audio + a visual "flat" tell on off-beat hits — the accents don't glow pink off-beat).
3. **Tighten & weight:** parry 130ms → ~100ms; add real strike recovery frames (commit); reduce air-control slightly so grounded feels planted. The heavy pocket.
4. **Punish up:** enemy hits cost more; fewer i-frames on stagger; but keep the telegraph lead-time *generous* — hard-but-fair means tight windows, loud tells.
5. **Mercy stays defenseless and now genuinely risky** — that's correct, don't soften it. Verify SET can still be *reached* in a hard room by a skilled player (the reconciliation depends on it being possible, just costly).

## Scope flag (the honest one)

Tight, deliberate, hard combat is **the single most tuning-expensive thing in the game.** Sekiro-feel is 90% invisible iteration — frame data, window sizes, recovery, telegraph readability, per-enemy rhythm. At a ~2-4hr scope this is achievable *if* we prove it on a tiny surface first and hold the bar. **Recommendation: M2 tunes exactly ONE enemy (the Auditioner, because it also carries the mercy-exit) and ONE boss (the Tonearm) to the full feel target before a single other enemy is built.** If those two feel like Sekiro-in-a-music-box, the identity is real and the roster inherits the tuning. If they don't, we learn it cheap. Do not build eight enemies at this difficulty bar on faith.
