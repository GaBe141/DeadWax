# DEAD WAX — World Canon (v1, 2026-08-08)

*Delivery layers: **[told]** = surface, any player gets it · **[played]** = heard via SET at depth · **[etched]** = layer-3, never stated outright — for the wiki-diggers.*

## Cosmology

**[told]** The world is a recording: a wax cylinder the size of a mountain, standing on end, groove winding down its inside. Long ago the Engineer cut the Voice — her master song — into it. Playback is life: a groove that gets played, speaks. The people are the song's tracks, given bodies to walk around in while they wait for their passage to come up.

**[played]** Sound in this world doesn't die when played — it *wears*. Every playback shaves something off. The Engineer rationed the Voice carefully: one playing, start to finish, over generations. The song had reached its middle when she died.

**[etched]** What did the Engineer record the Voice *from*? The runout etching is signed, but the signature isn't hers alone. There was a source. The game never says what. (Every recording is of something. Every bell is an answer. Let the forums bleed.)

## The Scratch — history's hinge

**[told]** The Engineer kept the world clean; dust was the only forbidden thing. When she died, dust gathered in the groove. Her stylus — you, Skip — hit a drift of it mid-bar and jumped: gouged a scratch across the world, deep enough to cut the song in half. Everyone above the Scratch had already been played. Everyone below it has never been heard.

**[told]** The people caught *at* the bar where it happened are the Stuck: looping their moment, over and over, because that is what a scratched record does. Grief, in this world, is a skip.

**[played]** The Tonearm lifted you off the wax before you could gouge twice, and has held you ever since — decades in its grip above the Label, the world's most careful hands around the world's most dangerous point. The game begins the night the world, still trying to play itself, stutters against the Scratch hard enough that the loop reaches the surface — and you step out of the arm.

**[played]** And Skip was not the first. The Engineer's first stylus — heavier, sharper, the finest player the world ever had — wore every groove he touched too much; being played by him was glorious and it was slow ruin. When he heard what he was, he retired himself: took the veil, took up the burnisher, never played again. She let him go, and cast a lighter second. He is still down there, smoothing. (See CAST.md: HUSH.)

**[etched]** The dust was not neglect. Dust is what a played world sheds — the Voice wearing out, as she always knew it would. The skip was always going to happen to whichever needle was in the groove when her rationing ran out. She knew. The etching says so.

## The three cultural facts (each one is a mechanic)

1. **Names are runtimes — and nicknames are wear.** A person is a passage of the Voice; the formal name is what the Engineer cut: form, key, timestamps. *"Adagio in C, 14:13–17:13."* But played = worn = loved, and a name is no different: hold it often enough and it wears smooth. *Addie.* A nickname is literally an act of playback — and it cannot be self-given; someone has to wear it into you. **[told]** So the naming convention is a lore system in disguise: the Label folk all go by worn names, while the Unplayed introduce themselves formally, in full, every time — *Fugue in D minor, 44:10 to 51:02* — because nobody ever called them anything shorter. Nobody ever called them at all. **[told]** Skip is the inversion: no runtime, no formal name — a stylus isn't a passage of the song. The Unplayed call the role **the Player**; the only *name* Skip owns is the one she wore into him. Her endearment was *little point*; what it wore down to, with use, was *Skip* — he hopped everywhere, and it was cute, and then he skipped, and the name and the disaster became one word. **[played]** The true name exists and is reserved for a late reveal, at the [etched] layer, beside the signature. **[etched]**
   Gravestones are **locked grooves** — endless one-bar loops at the cemetery, each playing its person's first note forever; the engraving is formal, but visitors chalk the worn name on the wax, and the chalk outlasts the visitors. SET at a locked groove to hear them. (Listening spots; Rite knowledge lives in what the graves still play.) **[told]** The end credits are the world's tracklist: every passage played on the descent, formal name first, worn name after where one was earned — including any the player's liner-book quietly gave to Unplayed that nobody else ever named. **[etched — never announced; discovered by finishing]**
2. **Bootlegging was forbidden.** Only the live Voice mattered; taping it was theft-of-wear. The Bootlegger did it anyway, obsessively, for years — and was exiled for it. Then the Scratch cut the played world off from its own past, and his contraband tapes became the only surviving copies of everything above the wound. The criminal is the archive now. (Your refrain tabs come from his stall.)
3. **Mourning is the next bar.** The rite for the Stuck was always known: learn the bar that *should have come next* in their moment, and play it to them. The loop completes, advances once, and ends. Nobody performs it anymore, because the one who taught it was the Engineer. (The Rites system, told to the player in the Bootlegger's own broken way.)

## The strata, as they were **[told]**

- **The Label** — the printed paper cap of the world: the town where the played retired. Type fades here; flyers advertise concerts that ended generations ago. Dry, spent, quiet. The great horn stands silent in the square, the Hound still sitting beside it.
- **The Overture** — the first-played grooves, worn nearly smooth. Ghost-thin echoes; the walls remember being music the way an old man remembers running.
- **The Unplayed** (below the Scratch) — the half of the song that never happened. The **Verse** lobe: narrow, wordy, dense with meaning nobody got to hear. The **Chorus** lobe: the hook — pools where the pressed sound loops on itself, trance-thick, easy to sink into and hard to leave.
- **The Undersong** — the deep arrangement: basslines, undertows, the parts of the song that hold the rest up. Liquid. You swim.
- **Dead Wax** — the runout: past the last note, the silent band where engineers scratch what they mean. The locked-groove graveyard is here, and the bent Spindle, and the Etching.

## The one who likes it broken

**[told at the Threshold, Act 3]** "Soon" is a title, not a name — the world's word for whoever holds the ending and cannot let it end. The seat outlives its holder; the next ticket is blank; Skip is the only blank thing in the world. The graffiti was never signed — it was addressed. And the game's final input honors it: the Last Bar is played by *holding* strike and **releasing** — the last input of the game is a release, held as long as the player dares. (Full staging: STORY.md, the Threshold + the Last Bar.)

**[told, Act 2 onward]** Something rose in the deep after the skip — not a cause of the wound, an *opportunist* of it: SOON, the hold-mark the Engineer wrote over the Last Bar, given a body and cheated of its cue when she died mid-gesture. It rules the unplayed as the false-hope king — *your moment is coming, stay ready, soon* — because if the song never finishes, its own moment never has to end before it begins. Its graffiti is one word. Its body is an unfinished sketch. Its palace is a waiting room. It is the only creature in the world grateful to Skip, and its gratitude is the most dangerous thing down there. **[played]** It is Stuck, at the highest level: holding one note for a conductor whose hand never fell. Its next bar is release. Its Rite is the cue. (Full entry: CAST.md.)

**The thesis triangle:** Hush says *never play them* — erasure as mercy. Soon says *never finish* — suspension as self-preservation. Skip says *let it end.* The game agrees with the kid.

## The thesis

**Grief is a skip. Mourning is the next bar.**

A loop is not a song. The Stuck are not evil and the Unplayed are not monsters; one is grief repeating and the other is longing unmet. Force works on both — a needle can shatter anything it can play — but the game's mercy verb is *completion*: let the bar advance, let the track be heard, let the song finish. The ending is not silence defeated. It is the quiet after a record ends, which is a different quiet than the one after a record breaks. The player should be able to feel the difference in one note.

## The endings

- **The Run [told]:** from the Scratch, ride the groove down at full speed — traversal is playback — playing every unheard passage as you pass. At the wound itself, the rival's burnisher smooths the Scratch flat — erasure given, at last, to the wound instead of the wounded; only he can do it, and only you gave him a reason to — and the song plays *through* where it broke, to the runout, to the etched last bar. The world finishes. The Unplayed get played. The good kind of quiet.
- **Force finish:** shatter the massed Unheard at the runout and strike the bar into a broken groove. Same note, ragged. The liner-book's last page is written in a shakier hand, and one line differs. (Cheap to build, heavy to receive.)

## Register rules (binding)

The liner-notes book carries the mania: track lists, defaced flyers, chalk tallies, marginalia at 2am energy. The world's spoken/played text carries the sorrow, played straight. The two never blend inside a Rite, a locked groove, or the ending — there the handwriting goes small and neat, because even the book knows where it is.
